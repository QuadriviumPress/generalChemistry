# Chapter 17 Solubility and Complexation Equilibriums

Although [Chapter 16 \"Aqueous Acid--Base Equilibriums\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16) focused exclusively on acid--base equilibriums in aqueous solutions, equilibrium concepts can also be applied to many other kinds of reactions that occur in aqueous solution. In this chapter, we describe the equilibriums involved in the solubility of ionic compounds and the formation of complex ions.

Solubility equilibriums involving ionic compounds are important in fields as diverse as medicine, biology, geology, and industrial chemistry. Carefully controlled precipitation reactions of calcium salts, for example, are used by many organisms to produce structural materials, such as bone and the shells that surround mollusks and bird eggs. In contrast, uncontrolled precipitation reactions of calcium salts are partially or wholly responsible for the formation of scale in coffee makers and boilers, "bathtub rings," and kidney stones, which can be excruciatingly painful. The principles discussed in this chapter will enable you to understand how these apparently diverse phenomena are related. Solubility equilibriums are also responsible for the formation of caves and their striking features, such as stalactites and stalagmites, through a long process involving the repeated dissolution and precipitation of calcium carbonate. In addition to all of these phenomena, by the end of this chapter you will understand why barium sulfate is ideally suited for x-ray imaging of the digestive tract, and why soluble complexes of gadolinium can be used for imaging soft tissue and blood vessels using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), even though most simple salts of both metals are toxic to humans.

## 17.1 Determining the Solubility of Ionic Compounds

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To calculate the solubility of an ionic compound from its *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

````````````

We begin our discussion of solubility and *complexation equilibriums*---those associated with the formation of complex ions---by developing quantitative methods for describing dissolution and precipitation reactions of ionic compounds in aqueous solution. Just as with acid--base equilibriums, we can describe the concentrations of ions in equilibrium with an ionic solid using an equilibrium constant expression.

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01)=

### The Solubility Product

When a slightly soluble ionic compound is added to water, some of it dissolves to form a solution, establishing an equilibrium between the pure solid and a solution of its ions. For the dissolution of calcium phosphate, one of the two main components of kidney stones, the equilibrium can be written as follows, with the solid salt on the left:[^chem-1]

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-eq01)=

Equation 17.1

$$\left. \text{Ca}_{3}{(\text{PO}_{4})}_{2}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons 3\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{PO}_{4}{}^{3 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

The equilibrium constant for the dissolution of a sparingly soluble salt is the solubility product (*K*<sub>sp</sub>)[^chem-2] of the salt. Because the concentration of a pure solid such as Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub> is a constant, it does not appear explicitly in the equilibrium constant expression. (For more information on the equilibrium constant expression, see [Chapter 15 \"Chemical Equilibrium\"](/chapters/ch-15-chemical-equilibrium.md#src-averill-1-0-ch15), [Section 15.2 \"The Equilibrium Constant\"](/chapters/ch-15-chemical-equilibrium.md#src-averill-1-0-ch15-s02).) The equilibrium constant expression for the dissolution of calcium phosphate is therefore

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-eq02)=

Equation 17.2

$$\begin{aligned}
K & {= \frac{{\lbrack\text{Ca}^{2 +}\rbrack}^{3}{\lbrack\text{PO}_{4}{}^{3 -}\rbrack}^{2}}{\lbrack\text{Ca}_{3}{(\text{PO}_{4})}_{2}\rbrack}} \\
{\lbrack\text{Ca}_{3}{(\text{PO}_{4})}_{2}\rbrack K} & {= K_{\text{sp}} = {\lbrack\text{Ca}^{2 +}\rbrack}^{3}{\lbrack\text{PO}_{4}{}^{3 -}\rbrack}^{2}}
\end{aligned}$$

At 25°C and pH 7.00, *K*<sub>sp</sub> for calcium phosphate is 2.07 × 10<sup>−33</sup>, indicating that the concentrations of Ca<sup>2+</sup> and PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3−</sup> ions in solution that are in equilibrium with solid calcium phosphate are very low. The values of *K*<sub>sp</sub> for some common salts are listed in [Table 17.1 \"Solubility Products for Selected Ionic Substances at 25°C\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-t01); they show that the magnitude of *K*<sub>sp</sub> varies dramatically for different compounds. Although *K*<sub>sp</sub> is not a function of pH in [Equation 17.1](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-eq01), changes in pH can affect the solubility of a compound, as you will discover in [Section 17.4 \"Solubility and pH\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04).

Solubility products are determined experimentally by directly measuring either the concentration of one of the component ions or the solubility of the compound in a given amount of water. However, whereas solubility is usually expressed in terms of *mass* of solute per 100 mL of solvent, *K*<sub>sp</sub>, like *K*, is defined in terms of the *molar* concentrations of the component ions.

### Example 1

Calcium oxalate monohydrate \[Ca(O<sub>2</sub>CCO<sub>2</sub>)·H<sub>2</sub>O, also written as CaC<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>·H<sub>2</sub>O\] is a sparingly soluble salt that is the other major component of kidney stones \[along with Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]. Its solubility in water at 25°C is 7.36 × 10<sup>−4</sup> g/100 mL. Calculate its *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

**Given:** solubility in g/100 mL

**Asked for:** *K* <sub>sp</sub>

**Strategy:**

**A** Write the balanced dissolution equilibrium and the corresponding solubility product expression.

**B** Convert the solubility of the salt to moles per liter. From the balanced dissolution equilibrium, determine the equilibrium concentrations of the dissolved solute ions. Substitute these values into the solubility product expression to calculate *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

**Solution:**

**A** We need to write the solubility product expression in terms of the concentrations of the component ions. For calcium oxalate monohydrate, the balanced dissolution equilibrium and the solubility product expression (abbreviating oxalate as ox<sup>2−</sup>) are as follows:

$$\left. \text{Ca}(\text{O}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2})\text{}\cdot \text{}\text{H}_{2}\text{O(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + {{}_{}^{-}\text{O}}{}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2}{}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)~~~~}K_{\text{sp}} = \lbrack\text{Ca}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{ox}^{2 -}\rbrack \right.$$

Neither solid calcium oxalate monohydrate nor water appears in the solubility product expression because their concentrations are essentially constant.

**B** Next we need to determine \[Ca<sup>2+</sup>\] and \[ox<sup>2−</sup>\] at equilibrium. We can use the mass of calcium oxalate monohydrate that dissolves in 100 mL of water to calculate the number of moles that dissolve in 100 mL of water. From this we can determine the number of moles that dissolve in 1.00 L of water. For dilute solutions, the density of the solution is nearly the same as that of water, so dissolving the salt in 1.00 L of water gives essentially 1.00 L of solution. Because each 1 mol of dissolved calcium oxalate monohydrate dissociates to produce 1 mol of calcium ions and 1 mol of oxalate ions, we can obtain the equilibrium concentrations that must be inserted into the solubility product expression. The number of moles of calcium oxalate monohydrate that dissolve in 100 mL of water is as follows:

$$\frac{7.36 \times 10^{- 4}\ \cancel{\text{g}}}{146.1\ \cancel{\text{g}}\text{/mol}} = 5.04 \times 10^{- 6}\text{~mol~Ca}(\text{O}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2})\text{}\cdot \text{}\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$

The number of moles of calcium oxalate monohydrate that dissolve in 1.00 L of the saturated solution is as follows:

$$\left( \frac{5.04 \times 10^{- 6}\text{~mol~Ca}(\text{O}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2}\text{}\cdot \text{})\text{H}_{2}\text{O}}{100\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} \right)\left( \frac{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}}{1.00\text{~L}} \right) = 5.04 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~mol/L} = 5.04 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~M}$$

Because of the stoichiometry of the reaction, the concentration of Ca<sup>2+</sup> and ox<sup>2−</sup> ions are both 5.04 × 10<sup>−5</sup> M. Inserting these values into the solubility product expression,

$$K_{\text{sp}} = \lbrack\text{Ca}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{ox}^{2 -}\rbrack = (5.04 \times 10^{- 5})(5.04 \times 10^{- 5}) = 2.54 \times 10^{- 9}$$

In our calculation, we have ignored the reaction of the weakly basic anion with water, which tends to make the actual solubility of many salts greater than the calculated value.

### The Ion Product

The ion product (*Q*)[^chem-3] of a salt is the product of the concentrations of the ions in solution raised to the same powers as in the solubility product expression. It is analogous to the reaction quotient (*Q*) discussed for gaseous equilibriums in [Chapter 15 \"Chemical Equilibrium\"](/chapters/ch-15-chemical-equilibrium.md#src-averill-1-0-ch15). Whereas *K*<sub>sp</sub> describes equilibrium concentrations, the ion product describes concentrations that are *not* necessarily equilibrium concentrations.

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

The ion product *Q* is analogous to the reaction quotient *Q* for gaseous equilibriums.

````````````

As summarized in [Figure 17.1 \"The Relationship between \"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s02-f01), there are three possible conditions for an aqueous solution of an ionic solid:

1.  *Q* **\<** *K*<sub>**sp**</sub>**.** The solution is unsaturated, and more of the ionic solid, if available, will dissolve.
2.  *Q* **=** *K*<sub>**sp**</sub>**.** The solution is saturated and at equilibrium.
3.  *Q* **\>** *K*<sub>**sp**</sub>**.** The solution is supersaturated, and ionic solid will precipitate.

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s02-f01)=

Figure 17.1 The Relationship between *Q* and *K*<sub>sp</sub>

![Figure 17.1 The Relationship between Q and K sp](/images/section_21/1ba98e3850116ed971e311d94d96df1e.jpg)

If *Q* is less than *K*<sub>sp</sub>, the solution is unsaturated and more solid will dissolve until the system reaches equilibrium (*Q* = *K*<sub>sp</sub>). If *Q* is greater than *K*<sub>sp</sub>, the solution is supersaturated and solid will precipitate until *Q* = *K*<sub>sp</sub>. If *Q* = *K*<sub>sp</sub>, the rate of dissolution is equal to the rate of precipitation; the solution is saturated, and no net change in the amount of dissolved solid will occur.

The process of calculating the value of the ion product and comparing it with the magnitude of the solubility product is a straightforward way to determine whether a solution is unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated. More important, the ion product tells chemists whether a precipitate will form when solutions of two soluble salts are mixed.

### Example 3

We mentioned that barium sulfate is used in medical imaging of the gastrointestinal tract. Its solubility product is 1.08 × 10<sup>−10</sup> at 25°C, so it is ideally suited for this purpose because of its low solubility when a "barium milkshake" is consumed by a patient. The pathway of the sparingly soluble salt can be easily monitored by x-rays. Will barium sulfate precipitate if 10.0 mL of 0.0020 M Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> is added to 100 mL of 3.2 × 10<sup>−4</sup> M BaCl<sub>2</sub>? Recall that NaCl is highly soluble in water.

**Given:** *K*<sub>sp</sub> and volumes and concentrations of reactants

**Asked for:** whether precipitate will form

**Strategy:**

**A** Write the balanced equilibrium equation for the precipitation reaction and the expression for *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

**B** Determine the concentrations of all ions in solution when the solutions are mixed and use them to calculate the ion product (*Q*).

**C** Compare the values of *Q* and *K*<sub>sp</sub> to decide whether a precipitate will form.

**Solution:**

**A** The only slightly soluble salt that can be formed when these two solutions are mixed is BaSO<sub>4</sub> because NaCl is highly soluble. The equation for the precipitation of BaSO<sub>4</sub> is as follows:

$$\left. \text{BaSO}_{4}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ba}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

The solubility product expression is as follows:

$$K_{\text{sp}} = \lbrack\text{Ba}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\rbrack = 1.08 \times 10^{- 10}$$

**B** To solve this problem, we must first calculate the ion product---*Q* = \[Ba<sup>2+</sup>\]\[SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>\]---using the concentrations of the ions that are present after the solutions are mixed and before any reaction occurs. The concentration of Ba<sup>2+</sup> when the solutions are mixed is the total number of moles of Ba<sup>2+</sup> in the original 100 mL of BaCl<sub>2</sub> solution divided by the final volume (100 mL + 10.0 mL = 110 mL):

$$\text{moles~Ba}^{2 +} = 100\ \cancel{\text{mL}}\left( \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} \right)\left( \frac{3.2 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} \right) = 3.2 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~mol~Ba}^{2 +}$$

$$\lbrack\text{Ba}^{2 +}\rbrack = \left( \frac{3.2 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~mol~Ba}^{2 +}}{110\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} \right)\left( \frac{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}}{1\text{~L}} \right) = 2.9 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~M~Ba}^{2 +}$$

Similarly, the concentration of SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup> after mixing is the total number of moles of SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup> in the original 10.0 mL of Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> solution divided by the final volume (110 mL):

$$\text{moles~SO}_{4}{}^{2 -} = 10.0\ \cancel{\text{mL}}\left( \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} \right)\left( \frac{0.0020\text{~mol}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}} \right) = 2.0 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~mol~SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}$$

$$\lbrack\text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\rbrack = \left( \frac{2.0 \times 10^{- 5}\text{~mol~SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}}{110\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} \right)\left( \frac{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}}{1\text{~L}} \right) = 1.8 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~M~SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}$$

We can now calculate *Q*:

$$Q = \lbrack\text{Ba}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\rbrack = (2.9 \times 10^{- 4})(1.8 \times 10^{- 4}) = 5.2 \times 10^{- 8}$$

**C** We now compare *Q* with the *K*<sub>sp</sub>. If *Q* \> *K*<sub>sp</sub>, then BaSO<sub>4</sub> will precipitate, but if *Q* \< *K*<sub>sp</sub>, it will not. Because *Q* \> *K*<sub>sp</sub>, we predict that BaSO<sub>4</sub> will precipitate when the two solutions are mixed. In fact, BaSO<sub>4</sub> will continue to precipitate until the system reaches equilibrium, which occurs when \[Ba<sup>2+</sup>\]\[SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>\] = *K*<sub>sp</sub> = 1.08 × 10<sup>−10</sup>.

### The Common Ion Effect and Solubility

The solubility product expression tells us that the equilibrium concentrations of the cation and the anion are inversely related. That is, as the concentration of the anion increases, the maximum concentration of the cation needed for precipitation to occur decreases---and vice versa---so that *K*<sub>sp</sub> is constant. Consequently, the solubility of an ionic compound depends on the concentrations of other salts that contain the same ions. This dependency is another example of the common ion effect discussed in [Chapter 16 \"Aqueous Acid--Base Equilibriums\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16), [Section 16.6 \"Buffers\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16-s06): adding a common cation or anion shifts a solubility equilibrium in the direction predicted by Le Châtelier's principle. As a result, *the solubility of any sparingly soluble salt is almost always decreased by the presence of a soluble salt that contains a common ion*.[^chem-4]

Consider, for example, the effect of adding a soluble salt, such as CaCl<sub>2</sub>, to a saturated solution of calcium phosphate \[Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]. We have seen that the solubility of Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub> in water at 25°C is 1.14 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M (*K*<sub>sp</sub> = 2.07 × 10<sup>−33</sup>). Thus a saturated solution of Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub> in water contains 3 × (1.14 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M) = 3.42 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M Ca<sup>2+</sup> and 2 × (1.14 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M) = 2.28 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3−</sup>, according to the stoichiometry shown in [Equation 17.1](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-eq01) (neglecting hydrolysis to form HPO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup> as described in [Chapter 16 \"Aqueous Acid--Base Equilibriums\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16)). If CaCl<sub>2</sub> is added to a saturated solution of Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>, the Ca<sup>2+</sup> ion concentration will increase such that \[Ca<sup>2+</sup>\] \> 3.42 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M, making *Q* \> *K*<sub>sp</sub>. The only way the system can return to equilibrium is for the reaction in [Equation 17.1](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01-s01-eq01) to proceed to the left, resulting in precipitation of Ca<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>. This will decrease the concentration of both Ca<sup>2+</sup> and PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3−</sup> until *Q* = *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

The common ion effect usually decreases the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt.
````````````

````````````{important} Summary

The equilibrium constant for a dissolution reaction, called the **solubility product (***K*<sub>**sp**</sub>**)**, is a measure of the solubility of a compound. Whereas solubility is usually expressed in terms of *mass* of solute per 100 mL of solvent, *K*<sub>sp</sub> is defined in terms of the *molar* concentrations of the component ions. In contrast, the **ion product (***Q***)** describes concentrations that are not necessarily equilibrium concentrations. Comparing *Q* and *K*<sub>sp</sub> enables us to determine whether a precipitate will form when solutions of two soluble salts are mixed. Adding a common cation or common anion to a solution of a sparingly soluble salt shifts the solubility equilibrium in the direction predicted by Le Châtelier's principle. The solubility of the salt is almost always decreased by the presence of a common ion.

````````````

````````````{important} Key Takeaway

- The solubility product (*K*<sub>sp</sub>) is used to calculate equilibrium concentrations of the ions in solution, whereas the ion product (*Q*) describes concentrations that are not necessarily at equilibrium.
````````````

## 17.2 Factors That Affect Solubility

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To understand the factors that determine the solubility of ionic compounds.

````````````

The solubility product of an ionic compound describes the concentrations of *ions* in equilibrium with a solid, but what happens if some of the cations become associated with anions rather than being completely surrounded by solvent? Then predictions of the total solubility of the compound based on the assumption that the solute exists solely as discrete ions would differ substantially from the actual solubility, as would predictions of ionic concentrations. In general, four situations explain why the solubility of a compound may be other than expected: ion pair formation, the incomplete dissociation of molecular solutes, the formation of complex ions, and changes in pH. The first two situations are described in this section, the formation of complex ions is discussed in [Section 17.3 \"The Formation of Complex Ions\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s03), and changes in pH are discussed in [Section 17.4 \"Solubility and pH\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04).

### Ion-Pair Formation

An ion pair[^chem-5] consists of a cation and an anion that are in intimate contact in solution, rather than separated by solvent ([Figure 17.2 \"Ion-Pair Formation\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s02-s01-f01)). The ions in an ion pair are held together by the same attractive electrostatic forces that we discussed in [Chapter 8 \"Ionic versus Covalent Bonding\"](/chapters/ch-08-ionic-versus-covalent-bonding.md#src-averill-1-0-ch08) for ionic solids. As a result, the ions in an ion pair migrate as a single unit, whose net charge is the sum of the charges on the ions. In many ways, we can view an ion pair as a species intermediate between the ionic solid (in which each ion participates in many cation--anion interactions that hold the ions in a rigid array) and the completely dissociated ions in solution (where each is fully surrounded by water molecules and free to migrate independently).

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s02-s01-f01)=

Figure 17.2 Ion-Pair Formation

![Figure 17.2 Ion-Pair Formation](/images/section_21/71c1bcefd12f44acdffda22c7ff1c40a.jpg)

In an ion pair, the cation and the anion are in intimate contact in solution and migrate as a single unit. They are not completely dissociated and individually surrounded by solvent molecules, as are the hydrated ions, which are free to migrate independently.

As illustrated for calcium sulfate in the following equation, a second equilibrium must be included to describe the solubility of salts that form ion pairs:

Equation 17.3

$$\left. \text{CaSO}_{4}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{}\cdot \text{}\underset{\text{ion~pair}}{\text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

The ion pair is represented by the symbols of the individual ions separated by a dot, which indicates that they are associated in solution. The formation of an ion pair is a dynamic process, just like any other equilibrium, so a particular ion pair may exist only briefly before dissociating into the free ions, each of which may later associate briefly with other ions.

Ion-pair formation can have a major effect on the measured solubility of a salt. For example, the measured *K*<sub>sp</sub> for calcium sulfate is 4.93 × 10<sup>−5</sup> at 25°C. The solubility of CaSO<sub>4</sub> should be 7.02 × 10<sup>−3</sup> M if the only equilibrium involved were as follows:

Equation 17.4

$$\left. \text{CaSO}_{4}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

In fact, the experimentally measured solubility of calcium sulfate at 25°C is 1.6 × 10<sup>−2</sup> M, almost twice the value predicted from its *K*<sub>sp</sub>. The reason for the discrepancy is that the concentration of ion pairs in a saturated CaSO<sub>4</sub> solution is almost as high as the concentration of the hydrated ions. Recall that the magnitude of attractive electrostatic interactions is greatest for small, highly charged ions. Hence ion pair formation is most important for salts that contain M<sup>2+</sup> and M<sup>3+</sup> ions, such as Ca<sup>2+</sup> and La<sup>3+</sup>, and is relatively unimportant for salts that contain monopositive cations, except for the smallest, Li<sup>+</sup>. We therefore expect a saturated solution of CaSO<sub>4</sub> to contain a high concentration of ion pairs and its solubility to be greater than predicted from its *K*<sub>sp</sub>.

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

The formation of ion pairs increases the solubility of a salt.

````````````

### Incomplete Dissociation

A molecular solute may also be more soluble than predicted by the measured concentrations of ions in solution due to *incomplete dissociation*. This is particularly common with weak organic acids. (For more information about weak organic acids, see [Chapter 16 \"Aqueous Acid--Base Equilibriums\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16)). Although strong acids (HA) dissociate completely into their constituent ions (H<sup>+</sup> and A<sup>−</sup>) in water, weak acids such as carboxylic acids do not (*K*<sub>a</sub> = 1.5 × 10<sup>−5</sup>). However, the molecular (undissociated) form of a weak acid (HA) is often quite soluble in water; for example, acetic acid (CH<sub>3</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H) is completely miscible with water. Many carboxylic acids, however, have only limited solubility in water, such as benzoic acid (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H), with *K*<sub>a</sub> = 6.25 × 10<sup>−5</sup>. Just as with calcium sulfate, we need to include an additional equilibrium to describe the solubility of benzoic acid:

Equation 17.5

$$\left. \text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{CO}_{2}\text{H(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{CO}_{2}\text{H(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{CO}_{2}{}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

In a case like this, measuring only the concentration of the ions grossly underestimates the total concentration of the organic acid in solution. In the case of benzoic acid, for example, the pH of a saturated solution at 25°C is 2.85, corresponding to \[H<sup>+</sup>\] = \[C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>CO<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>\] = 1.4 × 10<sup>−3</sup> M. The total concentration of benzoic acid in the solution, however, is 2.8 × 10<sup>−2</sup> M. Thus approximately 95% of the benzoic acid in solution is in the form of hydrated neutral molecules---C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H(aq)---and only about 5% is present as the dissociated ions ([Figure 17.3 \"Incomplete Dissociation of a Molecular Solute\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s02-s02-f01)).

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

Incomplete dissociation of a molecular solute that is miscible with water can increase the solubility of the solute.

````````````

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s02-s02-f01)=

Figure 17.3 Incomplete Dissociation of a Molecular Solute

![Figure 17.3 Incomplete Dissociation of a Molecular Solute](/images/section_21/ce4b16d52f003d5aedea4e8cea083d84.jpg)

In a saturated solution of benzoic acid in water at 25°C, only about 5% of the dissolved benzoic acid molecules are dissociated to form benzoate anions and hydrated protons. The remaining 95% exists in solution in the form of hydrated neutral molecules. (H<sub>2</sub>O molecules are omitted for clarity.)

Although ion pairs, such as Ca<sup>2+</sup>·SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>, and undissociated electrolytes, such as C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H, are both electrically neutral, there is a major difference in the forces responsible for their formation. Simple electrostatic attractive forces between the cation and the anion hold the ion pair together, whereas a polar covalent O−H bond holds together the undissociated electrolyte.

````````````{important} Summary

There are four explanations why the solubility of a compound can differ from the solubility indicated by the concentrations of ions: (1) **ion pair** formation, in which an anion and a cation are in intimate contact in solution and not separated by solvent, (2) the incomplete dissociation of molecular solutes, (3) the formation of complex ions, and (4) changes in pH. An ion pair is held together by electrostatic attractive forces between the cation and the anion, whereas incomplete dissociation results from intramolecular forces, such as polar covalent O--H bonds.

````````````

````````````{important} Key Takeaway

- Ion-pair formation, the incomplete dissociation of molecular solutes, the formation of complex ions, and changes in pH all affect solubility.

````````````

## 17.3 The Formation of Complex Ions

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To describe the formation of complex ions quantitatively.

````````````

In [Chapter 4 \"Reactions in Aqueous Solution\"](/chapters/ch-04-reactions-in-aqueous-solution.md#src-averill-1-0-ch04), you learned that metal ions in aqueous solution are *hydrated*---that is, surrounded by a shell of usually four or six water molecules. A hydrated ion is one kind of a complex ion[^chem-6] (or, simply, *complex*), a species formed between a central metal ion and one or more surrounding ligands[^chem-7], molecules or ions that contain at least one lone pair of electrons, such as the \[Al(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>3+</sup> ion in [Figure 16.12 \"Effect of a Metal Ion on the Acidity of Water\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16-s02-s05-f01).

A complex ion forms from a metal ion and a ligand because of a Lewis acid--base interaction. The positively charged metal ion acts as a Lewis acid, and the ligand, with one or more lone pairs of electrons, acts as a Lewis base. Small, highly charged metal ions, such as Cu<sup>2+</sup> or Ru<sup>3+</sup>, have the greatest tendency to act as Lewis acids, and consequently, they have the greatest tendency to form complex ions.

As an example of the formation of complex ions, consider the addition of ammonia to an aqueous solution of the hydrated Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion {\[Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup>}. Because it is a stronger base than H<sub>2</sub>O, ammonia replaces the water molecules in the hydrated ion to form the \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> ion. Formation of the \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> complex is accompanied by a dramatic color change, as shown in [Figure 17.4 \"The Formation of Complex Ions\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s03-f01). The solution changes from the light blue of \[Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> to the blue-violet characteristic of the \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> ion.

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s03-f01)=

Figure 17.4 The Formation of Complex Ions

![Figure 17.4 The Formation of Complex Ions](/images/section_21/ba2fa3e1313774b53eef3e71096d9a2c.jpg)

An aqueous solution of CuSO<sub>4</sub> consists of hydrated Cu<sup>2+</sup> ions in the form of pale blue \[Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> (left). The addition of aqueous ammonia to the solution results in the formation of the intensely blue-violet \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> ions, usually written as \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> ion (right) because ammonia, a stronger base than H<sub>2</sub>O, replaces water molecules from the hydrated Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion.

### The Formation Constant

The replacement of water molecules from \[Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> by ammonia occurs in sequential steps. Omitting the water molecules bound to Cu<sup>2+</sup> for simplicity, we can write the equilibrium reactions as follows:

Equation 17.6

$$\begin{array}{rlc}
{\text{Cu}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{NH}_{3}\text{(aq)}} & \left. \rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Cu}(\text{NH}_{3})\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{1} \\
{{\lbrack\text{Cu}(\text{NH}_{3})\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{NH}_{3}\text{(aq)}} & \left. \rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{2}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{2} \\
{{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{2}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{NH}_{3}\text{(aq)}} & \left. \rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{3}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{3} \\
{{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{3}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{NH}_{3}\text{(aq)}} & \left. \rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{4}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{4}
\end{array}$$

The sum of the stepwise reactions is the overall equation for the formation of the complex ion:[^chem-8]

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s03-s01-eq02)=

Equation 17.7

$$\left. \text{Cu}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{4NH}_{3}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{4}\rbrack}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

The equilibrium constant for the formation of the complex ion from the hydrated ion is called the formation constant (*K*<sub>f</sub>)[^chem-9]. The equilibrium constant expression for *K*<sub>f</sub> has the same general form as any other equilibrium constant expression. In this case, the expression is as follows:

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s03-s01-eq03)=

Equation 17.8

$$K_{\text{f}} = \frac{\left\lbrack {\lbrack\text{Cu}{(\text{NH}_{3})}_{4}\rbrack}^{2 +} \right\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{Cu}^{2 +}\rbrack{\lbrack\text{NH}_{3}\rbrack}^{4}} = 2.1 \times 10^{13} = K_{1}K_{2}K_{3}K_{4}$$

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

The formation constant (*K*<sub>f</sub>) has the same general form as any other equilibrium constant expression.

````````````

Water, a pure liquid, does not appear explicitly in the equilibrium constant expression, and the hydrated Cu<sup>2+</sup>(aq) ion is represented as Cu<sup>2+</sup> for simplicity. As for any equilibrium, the larger the value of the equilibrium constant (in this case, *K*<sub>f</sub>), the more stable the product. With *K*<sub>f</sub> = 2.1 × 10<sup>13</sup>, the \[Cu(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup> complex ion is very stable. ### The Effect of the Formation of Complex Ions on Solubility

What happens to the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt if a ligand that forms a stable complex ion is added to the solution? One such example occurs in conventional black-and-white photography, which was discussed briefly in [Chapter 4 \"Reactions in Aqueous Solution\"](/chapters/ch-04-reactions-in-aqueous-solution.md#src-averill-1-0-ch04).

Recall that black-and-white photographic film contains light-sensitive microcrystals of AgBr, or mixtures of AgBr and other silver halides. AgBr is a sparingly soluble salt, with a *K*<sub>sp</sub> of 5.35 × 10<sup>−13</sup> at 25°C. When the shutter of the camera opens, the light from the object being photographed strikes some of the crystals on the film and initiates a photochemical reaction that converts AgBr to black Ag metal. Well-formed, stable negative images appear in tones of gray, corresponding to the number of grains of AgBr converted, with the areas exposed to the most light being darkest. To fix the image and prevent more AgBr crystals from being converted to Ag metal during processing of the film, the unreacted AgBr on the film is removed using a complexation reaction to dissolve the sparingly soluble salt.

The reaction for the dissolution of silver bromide is as follows:

Equation 17.9

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{AgBr(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Br}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = 5.35 \times 10^{- 13}\text{~at~25°C}}
\end{array}$$

The equilibrium lies far to the left, and the equilibrium concentrations of Ag<sup>+</sup> and Br<sup>−</sup> ions are very low (7.31 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M). As a result, removing unreacted AgBr from even a single roll of film using pure water would require tens of thousands of liters of water and a great deal of time. Le Châtelier's principle tells us, however, that we can drive the reaction to the right by removing one of the products, which will cause more AgBr to dissolve. Bromide ion is difficult to remove chemically, but silver ion forms a variety of stable two-coordinate complexes with neutral ligands, such as ammonia, or with anionic ligands, such as cyanide or thiosulfate (S<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup>). In photographic processing, excess AgBr is dissolved using a concentrated solution of sodium thiosulfate.

![Chemical illustration from Chapter 17: Solubility and Complexation Equilibriums](/images/section_21/e4989f5a59931f16b35f7c79bf6e7673.jpg)

The reaction of Ag<sup>+</sup> with thiosulfate is as follows:

Equation 17.10

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Ag}{(\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3})}_{2}\rbrack}^{3 -}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{f}} = 2.9 \times 10^{13}}
\end{array}$$

The magnitude of the equilibrium constant indicates that almost all Ag<sup>+</sup> ions in solution will be immediately complexed by thiosulfate to form \[Ag(S<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>3−</sup>. We can see the effect of thiosulfate on the solubility of AgBr by writing the appropriate reactions and adding them together:

Equation 17.11

$$\begin{array}{rcl}
\left. \text{AgBr(s)}\rightleftharpoons\cancel{\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)}} + \text{Br}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{\text{sp}} & {= 5.35 \times 10^{- 13}} \\
\left. \cancel{\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)}} + 2\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Ag}{(\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3})}_{2}\rbrack}^{3 -}\text{(aq)} \right. & K_{\text{f}} & {= 2.9 \times 10^{13}} \\
\left. \text{AgBr(s)} + 2\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{Ag}{(\text{S}_{2}\text{O}_{3})}_{2}\rbrack}^{3 -}\text{(aq)} + \text{Br}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & K & {= K_{\text{sp}}K_{\text{f}} = 15}
\end{array}$$

Comparing *K* with *K*<sub>sp</sub> shows that the formation of the complex ion increases the solubility of AgBr by approximately 3 × 10<sup>13</sup>. The dramatic increase in solubility combined with the low cost and the low toxicity explains why sodium thiosulfate is almost universally used for developing black-and-white film. If desired, the silver can be recovered from the thiosulfate solution using any of several methods and recycled.

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

If a complex ion has a large *K*<sub>f</sub>, the formation of a complex ion can dramatically increase the solubility of sparingly soluble salts.
````````````

### Example 6

Due to the common ion effect, we might expect a salt such as AgCl to be much less soluble in a concentrated solution of KCl than in water. Such an assumption would be incorrect, however, because it ignores the fact that silver ion tends to form a two-coordinate complex with chloride ions (AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>). Calculate the solubility of AgCl in each situation:

1.  in pure water
2.  in 1.0 M KCl solution, ignoring the formation of any complex ions
3.  the same solution as in part (b) except taking the formation of complex ions into account, assuming that AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> is the only Ag<sup>+</sup> complex that forms in significant concentrations

At 25°C, *K*<sub>sp</sub> = 1.77 × 10<sup>−10</sup> for AgCl and *K*<sub>f</sub> = 1.1 × 10<sup>5</sup> for AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>.

**Given:** *K*<sub>sp</sub> of AgCl, *K*<sub>f</sub> of AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>, and KCl concentration

**Asked for:** solubility of AgCl in water and in KCl solution with and without the formation of complex ions

**Strategy:**

**A** Write the solubility product expression for AgCl and calculate the concentration of Ag<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup> in water.

**B** Calculate the concentration of Ag<sup>+</sup> in the KCl solution.

**C** Write balanced chemical equations for the dissolution of AgCl and for the formation of the AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> complex. Add the two equations and calculate the equilibrium constant for the overall equilibrium.

**D** Write the equilibrium constant expression for the overall reaction. Solve for the concentration of the complex ion.

**Solution:**

1.  **A** If we let *x* equal the solubility of AgCl, then at equilibrium \[Ag<sup>+</sup>\] = \[Cl<sup>−</sup>\] = *x* M. Substituting this value into the solubility product expression,

    $$\begin{array}{rcl}
    {K_{\text{sp}} =} & {\lbrack\text{Ag}^{+}\rbrack\lbrack\text{Cl}^{-}\rbrack} & {= (x)(x) = x^{2} = 1.77 \times 10^{- 10}} \\
     & x & {= 1.33 \times 10^{- 5}}
    \end{array}$$

    Thus the solubility of AgCl in pure water at 25°C is 1.33 × 10<sup>−5</sup> M.

2.  **B** If *x* equals the solubility of AgCl in the KCl solution, then at equilibrium \[Ag<sup>+</sup>\] = *x*  M and \[Cl<sup>−</sup>\] = (1.0 + *x*) M. Substituting these values into the solubility product expression and assuming that *x* \<\< 1.0,

    $$K_{\text{sp}} = \lbrack\text{Ag}^{+}\rbrack\lbrack\text{Cl}^{-}\rbrack = (x)(1.0 + x) \approx x(1.0) = 1.77 \times 10^{- 10} = x$$

    If the common ion effect were the only important factor, we would predict that AgCl is approximately five orders of magnitude less soluble in a 1.0 M KCl solution than in water.

3.  **C** To account for the effects of the formation of complex ions, we must first write the equilibrium equations for both the dissolution and the formation of complex ions. Adding the equations corresponding to *K*<sub>sp</sub> and *K*<sub>f</sub> gives us an equation that describes the dissolution of AgCl in a KCl solution. The equilibrium constant for the reaction is therefore the product of *K*<sub>sp</sub> and *K*<sub>f</sub>:

    $$\begin{array}{rcl}
    \left. \text{AgCl(s)}\rightleftharpoons\cancel{\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)}} + \cancel{\text{Cl}^{-}\text{(aq)}} \right. & K_{\text{sp}} & {= 1.77 \times 10^{- 10}} \\
    \left. \cancel{\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)}} + \cancel{2}\text{Cl}^{-}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{AgCl}_{2}\rbrack}^{-} \right. & K_{\text{f}} & {= 1.1 \times 10^{5}} \\
    \left. \text{AgCl(s)}\text{~+~}\text{Cl}^{-}\rightleftharpoons{\lbrack\text{AgCl}_{{}^{2}}\rbrack}^{-} \right. & K & {= K_{\text{sp}}K_{\text{f}} = 1.9 \times 10^{- 5}}
    \end{array}$$

    **D** If we let *x* equal the solubility of AgCl in the KCl solution, then at equilibrium \[AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>\] = *x* and \[Cl<sup>−</sup>\] = 1.0 − *x*. Substituting these quantities into the equilibrium constant expression for the net reaction and assuming that x \<\< 1.0,

    $$K = \frac{\lbrack\text{AgCl}_{2}{}^{-}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{Cl}^{-}\rbrack} = \frac{x}{1.0 - x} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{- 5} = x$$

    That is, AgCl dissolves in 1.0 M KCl to produce a 1.9 × 10<sup>−5</sup> M solution of the AgCl<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> complex ion. Thus we predict that AgCl has approximately the same solubility in a 1.0 M KCl solution as it does in pure water, which is 10<sup>5</sup> times greater than that predicted based on the common ion effect. (In fact, the measured solubility of AgCl in 1.0 M KCl is almost a factor of 10 *greater* than that in pure water, largely due to the formation of other chloride-containing complexes.)

## 17.4 Solubility and pH

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To understand why the solubility of many compounds depends on pH.

````````````

The solubility of many compounds depends strongly on the pH of the solution. For example, the anion in many sparingly soluble salts is the conjugate base of a weak acid that may become protonated in solution. In addition, the solubility of simple binary compounds such as oxides and sulfides, both strong bases, is often dependent on pH. In this section, we discuss the relationship between the solubility of these classes of compounds and pH.

### The Effect of Acid--Base Equilibriums on the Solubility of Salts

We begin our discussion by examining the effect of pH on the solubility of a representative salt, M<sup>+</sup>A<sup>−</sup>, where A<sup>−</sup> is the conjugate base of the weak acid HA. When the salt dissolves in water, the following reaction occurs:

Equation 17.13

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{MA(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{M}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{A}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = \lbrack\text{M}^{+}\rbrack\lbrack\text{A}^{-}\rbrack}
\end{array}$$

The anion can also react with water in a hydrolysis reaction:

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s01-eq02)=

Equation 17.14

$$\left. \text{A}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}\rightleftharpoons\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{HA(aq)} \right.$$

Because of the reaction described in [Equation 17.14](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s01-eq02), the predicted solubility of a sparingly soluble salt that has a basic anion such as S<sup>2−</sup>, PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3−</sup>, or CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup> is increased, as described in [Section 17.1 \"Determining the Solubility of Ionic Compounds\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s01). If instead a strong acid is added to the solution, the added H<sup>+</sup> will react essentially completely with A<sup>−</sup> to form HA. This reaction decreases \[A<sup>−</sup>\], which decreases the magnitude of the ion product (*Q* = \[M<sup>+</sup>\]\[A<sup>−</sup>\]). According to Le Châtelier's principle, more MA will dissolve until *Q* = *K*<sub>sp</sub>. Hence *an acidic pH dramatically increases the solubility of virtually all sparingly soluble salts whose anion is the conjugate base of a weak acid*. In contrast, pH has little to no effect on the solubility of salts whose anion is the conjugate base of a stronger weak acid or a *strong* acid, respectively (e.g., chlorides, bromides, iodides, and sulfates). For example, the hydroxide salt Mg(OH)<sub>2</sub> is relatively insoluble in water:

Equation 17.15

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{Mg(OH)}_{2}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Mg}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = 5.61 \times 10^{- 12}}
\end{array}$$

When acid is added to a saturated solution that contains excess solid Mg(OH)<sub>2</sub>, the following reaction occurs, removing OH<sup>−</sup> from solution:

Equation 17.16

$$\left. \text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \right.$$

The overall equation for the reaction of Mg(OH)<sub>2</sub> with acid is thus

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s01-eq05)=

Equation 17.17

$$\left. \text{Mg(OH)}_{\text{2}}\text{(s)} + \text{2H}^{+}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Mg}^{\text{2+}}\text{(aq)} + \text{2H}_{\text{2}}\text{O(l)} \right.$$

As more acid is added to a suspension of Mg(OH)<sub>2</sub>, the equilibrium shown in [Equation 17.17](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s01-eq05) is driven to the right, so more Mg(OH)<sub>2</sub> dissolves.

Such pH-dependent solubility is not restricted to salts that contain anions derived from water. For example, CaF<sub>2</sub> is a sparingly soluble salt:

Equation 17.18

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{CaF}_{2}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Ca}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{F}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = 3.45 \times 10^{- 11}}
\end{array}$$

When strong acid is added to a saturated solution of CaF<sub>2</sub>, the following reaction occurs:

Equation 17.19

$$\left. \text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{F}^{-}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{HF(aq)} \right.$$

Because the forward reaction decreases the fluoride ion concentration, more CaF<sub>2</sub> dissolves to relieve the stress on the system. The net reaction of CaF<sub>2</sub> with strong acid is thus

Equation 17.20

CaF<sub>2</sub>(s) + 2H<sup>+</sup>(aq) → Ca<sup>2+</sup>(aq) + 2HF(aq)

````````````{important} Note the Pattern
Sparingly soluble salts derived from weak acids tend to be more soluble in an acidic solution.
````````````

### Example 7

Lead oxalate (PbC<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>), lead iodide (PbI<sub>2</sub>), and lead sulfate (PbSO<sub>4</sub>) are all rather insoluble, with *K*<sub>sp</sub> values of 4.8 × 10<sup>−10</sup>, 9.8 × 10<sup>−9</sup>, and 2.53 × 10<sup>−8</sup>, respectively. What effect does adding a strong acid, such as perchloric acid, have on their relative solubilities?

**Given:** *K*<sub>sp</sub> values for three compounds

**Asked for:** relative solubilities in acid solution

**Strategy:**

Write the balanced chemical equation for the dissolution of each salt. Because the strongest conjugate base will be most affected by the addition of strong acid, determine the relative solubilities from the relative basicity of the anions.

**Solution:**

The solubility equilibriums for the three salts are as follows:

$$\begin{array}{r}
\left. \text{PbC}_{2}\text{O}_{4}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Pb}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{C}_{2}\text{O}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right. \\
\left. \text{PbI}_{2}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Pb}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. \\
\left. \text{PbSO}_{4}\text{(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Pb}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.
\end{array}$$

The addition of a strong acid will have the greatest effect on the solubility of a salt that contains the conjugate base of a weak acid as the anion. Because HI is a strong acid, we predict that adding a strong acid to a saturated solution of PbI<sub>2</sub> will not greatly affect its solubility; the acid will simply dissociate to form H<sup>+</sup>(aq) and the corresponding anion. In contrast, oxalate is the fully deprotonated form of oxalic acid (HO<sub>2</sub>CCO<sub>2</sub>H), which is a weak diprotic acid (p*K*<sub>a1</sub> = 1.23 and p*K*<sub>a2</sub> = 4.19). Consequently, the oxalate ion has a significant affinity for one proton and a lower affinity for a second proton. Adding a strong acid to a saturated solution of lead oxalate will result in the following reactions:

$$\begin{array}{r}

### Acidic, Basic, and Amphoteric Oxides and Hydroxides

One of the earliest classifications of substances was based on their solubility in acidic versus basic solution, which led to the classification of oxides and hydroxides as being either basic or acidic. Basic oxides[^chem-10] and hydroxides either react with water to produce a basic solution or dissolve readily in aqueous acid. Acidic oxides[^chem-11] or hydroxides either react with water to produce an acidic solution or are soluble in aqueous base. As shown in [Figure 17.8 \"Classification of the Oxides of the Main Group Elements According to Their Acidic or Basic Character\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s02-f01), there is a clear correlation between the acidic or the basic character of an oxide and the position of the element combined with oxygen in the periodic table. *Oxides of metallic elements are generally basic oxides, and oxides of nonmetallic elements are acidic oxides.* Compare, for example, the reactions of a typical metal oxide, cesium oxide, and a typical nonmetal oxide, sulfur trioxide, with water:

Equation 17.25

$$\left. \text{Cs}_{2}\text{O(s)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}\rightarrow\text{2Cs}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

Equation 17.26

$$\left. \text{SO}_{3}\text{(g)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

Cesium oxide reacts with water to produce a basic solution of cesium hydroxide, whereas sulfur trioxide reacts with water to produce a solution of sulfuric acid---very different behaviors indeed!

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

Metal oxides generally react with water to produce basic solutions, whereas nonmetal oxides produce acidic solutions.

````````````

The difference in reactivity is due to the difference in bonding in the two kinds of oxides. Because of the low electronegativity of the metals at the far left in the periodic table, their oxides are best viewed as containing discrete M*n*<sup>+</sup> cations and O<sup>2−</sup> anions. At the other end of the spectrum are nonmetal oxides; due to their higher electronegativities, nonmetals form oxides with covalent bonds to oxygen. Because of the high electronegativity of oxygen, however, the covalent bond between oxygen and the other atom, E, is usually polarized: E<sup>δ+</sup>--O<sup>δ−</sup>. The atom E in these oxides acts as a Lewis acid that reacts with the oxygen atom of water to produce an oxoacid. Oxides of metals in high oxidation states also tend to be acidic oxides for the same reason: they contain covalent bonds to oxygen. An example of an acidic metal oxide is MoO<sub>3</sub>, which is insoluble in both water and acid but dissolves in strong base to give solutions of the molybdate ion (MoO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>):

Equation 17.27

MoO<sub>3</sub>(s) + 2OH<sup>−</sup>(aq) → MoO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l)

As shown in [Figure 17.8 \"Classification of the Oxides of the Main Group Elements According to Their Acidic or Basic Character\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s02-f01), there is a gradual transition from basic metal oxides to acidic nonmetal oxides as we go from the lower left to the upper right in the periodic table, with a broad diagonal band of oxides of intermediate character separating the two extremes. Many of the oxides of the elements in this diagonal region of the periodic table are soluble in both acidic *and* basic solutions; consequently, they are called amphoteric oxides[^chem-12] (from the Greek *ampho*, meaning "both," as in *amphiprotic*, which was defined in [Chapter 16 \"Aqueous Acid--Base Equilibriums\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16), [Section 16.1 \"The Autoionization of Water\"](/chapters/ch-16-aqueous-acid-base-equilibriums.md#src-averill-1-0-ch16-s01)). Amphoteric oxides either dissolve in acid to produce water or dissolve in base to produce a soluble complex. As shown in [Figure 17.9 \"Chromium(III) Hydroxide \[Cr(OH)\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s02-f02), for example, mixing the amphoteric oxide Cr(OH)<sub>3</sub> (also written as Cr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>·3H<sub>2</sub>O) with water gives a muddy, purple-brown suspension. Adding acid causes the Cr(OH)<sub>3</sub> to dissolve to give a bright violet solution of Cr<sup>3+</sup>(aq), which contains the \[Cr(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>3+</sup> ion, whereas adding strong base gives a green solution of the \[Cr(OH)<sub>4</sub>\]<sup>−</sup> ion. The chemical equations for the reactions are as follows:

Equation 17.28

$$\left. \text{Cr(OH)}_{3}\text{(s)} + 3\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}\rightarrow\underset{\text{violet}}{\text{Cr}^{3 +}\text{(aq)~}} + 3\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \right.$$

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s02-eq05)=

Equation 17.29

$$\left. \text{Cr(OH)}_{3}\text{(s)} + \text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}\rightarrow\underset{\text{green}}{{\lbrack\text{Cr(OH)}_{4}\rbrack}^{-}}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

### Selective Precipitation Using pH

Many dissolved metal ions can be separated by the selective precipitation of the cations from solution under specific conditions. In this technique, pH is often used to control the concentration of the anion in solution, which controls which cations precipitate.

````````````{important} Note the Pattern

The concentration of anions in solution can often be controlled by adjusting the pH, thereby allowing the selective precipitation of cations.

````````````

Suppose, for example, we have a solution that contains 1.0 mM Zn<sup>2+</sup> and 1.0 mM Cd<sup>2+</sup> and want to separate the two metals by selective precipitation as the insoluble sulfide salts, ZnS and CdS. The relevant solubility equilibriums can be written as follows:

Equation 17.30

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{ZnS(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Zn}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{S}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = 1.6 \times 10^{- 24}}
\end{array}$$

Equation 17.31

$$\begin{array}{rc}
\left. \text{CdS(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{Cd}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{S}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right. & {K_{\text{sp}} = 8.0 \times 10^{- 27}}
\end{array}$$

Because the S<sup>2−</sup> ion is quite basic and reacts extensively with water to give HS<sup>−</sup> and OH<sup>−</sup>, the solubility equilibriums are more accurately written as $\left. \text{MS(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{M}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{HS}^{-}(\text{aq)} + \text{OH}^{-} \right.$ rather than $\left. \text{MS(s)}\rightleftharpoons\text{M}^{2 +}\text{(aq)} + \text{S}^{2 -}\text{(aq)}\text{.} \right.$ Here we use the simpler form involving S<sup>2−</sup>, which is justified because we take the reaction of S<sup>2−</sup> with water into account later in the solution, arriving at the same answer using either equilibrium equation.

The sulfide concentrations needed to cause ZnS and CdS to precipitate are as follows:

Equation 17.32

$$\begin{aligned}
K_{\text{sp}} & {= \lbrack\text{Zn}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack} \\
{1.6 \times 10^{- 24}} & {= (0.0010\text{~M})\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack} \\
{1.6 \times 10^{- 21}\text{~M}} & {= \lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack}
\end{aligned}$$

Equation 17.33

$$\begin{aligned}
K_{\text{sp}} & {= \lbrack\text{Cd}^{2 +}\rbrack\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack} \\
{8.0 \times 10^{- 27}} & {= (0.0010\text{~M})\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack} \\
{8.0 \times 10^{- 24}\text{~M}} & {= \lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack}
\end{aligned}$$

Thus sulfide concentrations between 1.6 × 10<sup>−21</sup> M and 8.0 × 10<sup>−24</sup> M will precipitate CdS from solution but not ZnS. How do we obtain such low concentrations of sulfide? A saturated aqueous solution of H<sub>2</sub>S contains 0.10 M H<sub>2</sub>S at 20°C. The p*K*<sub>a1</sub> for H<sub>2</sub>S is 6.97, and p*K*<sub>a2</sub> corresponding to the formation of \[S<sup>2−</sup>\] is 12.90. The equations for these reactions are as follows:

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s03-eq05)=

Equation 17.34

$$\begin{array}{rcc}
\left. \text{H}_{2}\text{S(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{HS}^{-}\text{(aq)} \right. & {\text{p}K_{\text{a1}} = 6.97\text{,}} & {K_{\text{a1}} = 1.1 \times 10^{- 7}} \\
\left. \text{HS}^{-}\text{(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{S}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right. & {\text{p}K_{\text{a}2} = 12.90\text{,}} & {K_{\text{a}2} = 1.3 \times 10^{- 13}}
\end{array}$$

We can show that the concentration of S<sup>2−</sup> is 1.3 × 10<sup>−13</sup> by comparing *K*<sub>a1</sub> and *K*<sub>a2</sub> and recognizing that the contribution to \[H<sup>+</sup>\] from the dissociation of HS<sup>−</sup> is negligible compared with \[H<sup>+</sup>\] from the dissociation of H<sub>2</sub>S. Thus substituting 0.10 M in the equation for *K*<sub>a1</sub> for the concentration of H<sub>2</sub>S, which is essentially constant regardless of the pH, gives the following:

Equation 17.35

$$\begin{array}{rc}
{K_{\text{a}1} = 1.1 \times 10^{- 7} = \frac{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack\lbrack\text{HS}^{-}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{H}_{2}\text{S}\rbrack}} & {= \frac{x^{2}}{0.10\text{~M}}} \\
 & {x = 1.1 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~M} = \lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack = \lbrack\text{HS}^{-}\rbrack}
\end{array}$$

Substituting this value for \[H<sup>+</sup>\] and \[HS<sup>−</sup>\] into the equation for *K*<sub>a2</sub>,

$$K_{\text{a2}} = 1.3 \times 10^{- 13} = \frac{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{HS}^{-}\rbrack} = \frac{(1.1 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~M})x}{1.1 \times 10^{- 4}\text{~M}} = x = \lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack$$

Although \[S<sup>2−</sup>\] in an H<sub>2</sub>S solution is very low (1.3 × 10<sup>−13</sup> M), bubbling H<sub>2</sub>S through the solution until it is saturated would precipitate both metal ions because the concentration of S<sup>2−</sup> would then be much greater than 1.6 × 10<sup>−21</sup> M. Thus we must adjust \[S<sup>2−</sup>\] to stay within the desired range. The most direct way to do this is to adjust \[H<sup>+</sup>\] by adding acid to the H<sub>2</sub>S solution (recall Le Châtelier\'s principle), thereby driving the equilibrium in [Equation 17.34](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s04-s03-eq05) to the left. The overall equation for the dissociation of H<sub>2</sub>S is as follows:

Equation 17.36

$$\left. \text{H}_{2}\text{S(aq)}\rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{S}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

Now we can use the equilibrium constant *K* for the overall reaction, which is the product of *K*<sub>a1</sub> and *K*<sub>a2</sub>, and the concentration of H<sub>2</sub>S in a saturated solution to calculate the H<sup>+</sup> concentration needed to produce \[S<sup>2−</sup>\] of 1.6 × 10<sup>−21</sup> M:

Equation 17.37

$$K = K_{\text{a1}}K_{\text{a2}} = (1.1 \times 10^{- 7})(1.3 \times 10^{- 13}) = 1.4 \times 10^{- 20} = \frac{{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack}^{2}\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{H}_{2}\text{S}\rbrack}$$

Equation 17.38

$$\begin{array}{r}
{{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack}^{2} = \frac{K\lbrack\text{H}_{2}\text{S}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{S}^{2 -}\rbrack} = \frac{(1.4 \times 10^{- 20})(0.10\text{~M})}{1.6 \times 10^{- 21}\text{~M}} = 0.88} \\
{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack = 0.94}
\end{array}$$

Thus adding a strong acid such as HCl to make the solution 0.94 M in H<sup>+</sup> will prevent the more soluble ZnS from precipitating while ensuring that the less soluble CdS will precipitate when the solution is saturated with H<sub>2</sub>S.

{\lbrack\text{ox}^{2 -}\rbrack} & {= 2.9 \times 10^{- 8}\text{~M}}
\end{aligned}$$

Thus lanthanum oxalate is less soluble and will selectively precipitate when the oxalate concentration is between 2.9 × 10<sup>−8</sup> M and 2.32 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M.

**B** To prevent Ca<sup>2+</sup> from precipitating as calcium oxalate, we must add enough H<sup>+</sup> to give a maximum oxalate concentration of 2.32 × 10<sup>−7</sup> M. We can calculate the required \[H<sup>+</sup>\] by using the overall equation for the dissociation of oxalic acid to oxalate:

$$\left. \text{HO}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2}\text{H(aq)}\rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{C}_{2}\text{O}_{4}{}^{2 -}\text{(aq)} \right.$$

$$K = K_{\text{a1}}K_{\text{a2}} = (10^{- 1.25})(10^{- 3.81}) = 10^{- 5.06} = 8.7 \times 10^{- 6}$$

Substituting the desired oxalate concentration into the equilibrium constant expression,

$$\begin{array}{rcl}
{8.7 \times 10^{- 6}} & {= \frac{{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack}^{2}\lbrack\text{ox}^{2 -}\rbrack}{\lbrack\text{HO}_{2}\text{CCO}_{2}\text{H}\rbrack}} & {= \frac{{\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack}^{2}(2.32 \times 10^{- 7})}{1.0}} \\
 & {\lbrack\text{H}^{+}\rbrack} & {= 6.1\text{~M}}
\end{array}$$

Thus adding enough HCl to give \[H<sup>+</sup>\] = 6.1 M will cause only La<sub>2</sub>(C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>)<sub>3</sub>·9H<sub>2</sub>O to precipitate from the solution.

````````````{important} Summary

The anion in many sparingly soluble salts is the conjugate base of a weak acid. At low pH, protonation of the anion can dramatically increase the solubility of the salt. Oxides can be classified as acidic oxides or basic oxides. **Acidic oxides** either react with water to give an acidic solution or dissolve in strong base; most acidic oxides are nonmetal oxides or oxides of metals in high oxidation states. **Basic oxides** either react with water to give a basic solution or dissolve in strong acid; most basic oxides are oxides of metallic elements. Oxides or hydroxides that are soluble in both acidic and basic solutions are called **amphoteric oxides**. Most elements whose oxides exhibit amphoteric behavior are located along the diagonal line separating metals and nonmetals in the periodic table. In solutions that contain mixtures of dissolved metal ions, the pH can be used to control the anion concentration needed to selectively precipitate the desired cation.
````````````

## 17.5 Qualitative Analysis Using Selective Precipitation

````````````{note} Learning Objective

1.  To know how to separate metal ions by selective precipitation.

````````````

The composition of relatively complex mixtures of metal ions can be determined using qualitative analysis[^chem-13], a procedure for discovering the *identity* of metal ions present in the mixture (rather than quantitative information about their amounts).

The procedure used to separate and identify more than 20 common metal cations from a single solution consists of selectively precipitating only a few kinds of metal ions at a time under given sets of conditions. Consecutive precipitation steps become progressively *less* selective until almost all of the metal ions are precipitated, as illustrated in [Figure 17.10 \"Steps in a Typical Qualitative Analysis Scheme for a Solution That Contains Several Metal Ions\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s05-f01).

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s05-f01)=

Figure 17.10 Steps in a Typical Qualitative Analysis Scheme for a Solution That Contains Several Metal Ions

![Figure 17.10 Steps in a Typical Qualitative Analysis Scheme for a Solution That Contains Several Metal Ions](/images/section_21/80b10bacacc595497de1ee392447ca28.jpg)

### Group 1: Insoluble Chlorides

Most metal chloride salts are soluble in water; only Ag<sup>+</sup>, Pb<sup>2+</sup>, and Hg<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup> form chlorides that precipitate from water. Thus the first step in a qualitative analysis is to add about 6 M HCl, thereby causing AgCl, PbCl<sub>2</sub>, and/or Hg<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> to precipitate. If no precipitate forms, then these cations are not present in significant amounts. The precipitate can be collected by filtration or centrifugation.

### Group 2: Acid-Insoluble Sulfides

Next, the acidic solution is saturated with H<sub>2</sub>S gas. Only those metal ions that form very insoluble sulfides, such as As<sup>3+</sup>, Bi<sup>3+</sup>, Cd<sup>2+</sup>, Cu<sup>2+</sup>, Hg<sup>2+</sup>, Sb<sup>3+</sup>, and Sn<sup>2+</sup>, precipitate as their sulfide salts under these acidic conditions. All others, such as Fe<sup>2+</sup> and Zn<sup>2+</sup>, remain in solution. Once again, the precipitates are collected by filtration or centrifugation.

### Group 3: Base-Insoluble Sulfides (and Hydroxides)

Ammonia or NaOH is now added to the solution until it is basic, and then (NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>S is added. This treatment removes any remaining cations that form insoluble hydroxides or sulfides. The divalent metal ions Co<sup>2+</sup>, Fe<sup>2+</sup>, Mn<sup>2+</sup>, Ni<sup>2+</sup>, and Zn<sup>2+</sup> precipitate as their sulfides, and the trivalent metal ions Al<sup>3+</sup> and Cr<sup>3+</sup> precipitate as their hydroxides: Al(OH)<sub>3</sub> and Cr(OH)<sub>3</sub>. If the mixture contains Fe<sup>3+</sup>, sulfide reduces the cation to Fe<sup>2+</sup>, which precipitates as FeS.

### Group 4: Insoluble Carbonates or Phosphates

The next metal ions to be removed from solution are those that form insoluble carbonates and phosphates. When Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> is added to the basic solution that remains after the precipitated metal ions are removed, insoluble carbonates precipitate and are collected. Alternatively, adding (NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> causes the same metal ions to precipitate as insoluble phosphates.

### Group 5: Alkali Metals

At this point, we have removed all the metal ions that form water-insoluble chlorides, sulfides, carbonates, or phosphates. The only common ions that might remain are any alkali metals (Li<sup>+</sup>, Na<sup>+</sup>, K<sup>+</sup>, Rb<sup>+</sup>, and Cs<sup>+</sup>) and ammonium (NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>). We now take a second sample from the *original* solution and add a small amount of NaOH to neutralize the ammonium ion and produce NH<sub>3</sub>. (We cannot use the same sample we used for the first four groups because we added ammonium to that sample in earlier steps.) Any ammonia produced can be detected by either its odor or a litmus paper test. A flame test on another original sample is used to detect sodium, which produces a characteristic bright yellow color. As discussed in [Chapter 6 \"The Structure of Atoms\"](/chapters/ch-06-the-structure-of-atoms.md#src-averill-1-0-ch06), the other alkali metal ions also give characteristic colors in flame tests, which allows them to be identified if only one is present.

Metal ions that precipitate together are separated by various additional techniques, such as forming complex ions, changing the pH of the solution, or increasing the temperature to redissolve some of the solids. For example, the precipitated metal chlorides of group 1 cations, containing Ag<sup>+</sup>, Pb<sup>2+</sup>, and Hg<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup>, are all quite insoluble in water. Because PbCl<sub>2</sub> is much more soluble in hot water than are the other two chloride salts, however, adding water to the precipitate and heating the resulting slurry will dissolve any PbCl<sub>2</sub> present. Isolating the solution and adding a small amount of Na<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> solution to it will produce a bright yellow precipitate of PbCrO<sub>4</sub> if Pb<sup>2+</sup> was in the original sample ([Figure 17.11 \"The Separation of Metal Ions from Group 1 Using Qualitative Analysis\"](/chapters/ch-17-solubility-and-complexation-eq.md#src-averill-1-0-ch17-s05-s05-f01)).

As another example, treating the precipitates from group 1 cations with aqueous ammonia will dissolve any AgCl because Ag<sup>+</sup> forms a stable complex with ammonia: \[Ag(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>+</sup>. In addition, Hg<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> *disproportionates* in ammonia (2Hg<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup> → Hg + Hg<sup>2+</sup>) to form a black solid that is a mixture of finely divided metallic mercury and an insoluble mercury(II) compound, which is separated from solution:

Equation 17.39

Hg<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub>(s) + 2NH<sub>3</sub>(aq) → Hg(l) + Hg(NH<sub>2</sub>)Cl(s) + NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>(aq) + Cl<sup>−</sup>(aq)

(src-averill-1-0-ch17-s05-s05-f01)=

Figure 17.11 The Separation of Metal Ions from Group 1 Using Qualitative Analysis

![Figure 17.11 The Separation of Metal Ions from Group 1 Using Qualitative Analysis](/images/section_21/811fa4e9ba09bd16335da299bb692da8.jpg)

In (a), the cations of group 1 precipitate when HCl(aq) is added to a solution containing a mixture of cations. (b) When a small amount of Na<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> solution is added to a sample containing Pb<sup>2+</sup> ions in water, a bright yellow precipitate of PbCrO<sub>4</sub> forms. (c) Adding aqueous ammonia to a second portion of the solid sample produces a black solid that is a mixture of finely divided metallic mercury, an insoluble mercury(II) compound \[Hg(NH<sub>2</sub>)Cl\], and a stable \[Ag(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>+</sup>(aq) complex. (d) The presence of Ag<sup>+</sup> is detected by decanting the solution from the precipitated mercury and mercury complex and adding hydrochloric acid to the decanted solution, which causes AgCl to precipitate.

Any silver ion in the solution is then detected by adding HCl, which reverses the reaction and gives a precipitate of white AgCl that slowly darkens when exposed to light:

Equation 17.40

\[Ag(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>\]<sup>+</sup>(aq) + 2H<sup>+</sup>(aq) + Cl<sup>−</sup>(aq) → AgCl(s) + 2NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>(aq)

Similar but slightly more complex reactions are also used to separate and identify the individual components of the other groups.

````````````{important} Summary

In **qualitative analysis**, the identity, not the amount, of metal ions present in a mixture is determined. The technique consists of selectively precipitating only a few kinds of metal ions at a time under given sets of conditions. Consecutive precipitation steps become progressively *less* selective until almost all the metal ions are precipitated. Other additional steps are needed to separate metal ions that precipitate together.

````````````

````````````{important} Key Takeaway

- Several common metal cations can be identified in a solution using selective precipitation.
````````````
